The challenge

ePlanner had been built incrementally by multiple engineering squads over several years, with no shared design system and no dedicated designer overseeing the whole product. The result was a platform where each module worked differently — components that looked the same behaved differently, navigation patterns didn't carry across tools, and interaction inconsistencies had accumulated without anyone with the mandate to address them.

The users (urban planners, architects, government analysts) are domain experts, not software experts. They have adapted to the inconsistencies through workarounds: memorising sequences, exporting data to check it elsewhere, re-running simulations to verify things the interface should have made clear. The problems weren't dramatic; they were the kind of friction that adds up quietly over time.

I was brought in as the first designer with a mandate to work across the whole product, not just individual features.

What I owned

I was embedded directly within URA's Digital and Planning Lab as the sole designer, not as an external consultant handing off deliverables, but as part of the internal team. Full ownership of design decisions, direct access to stakeholders and end users, and accountability for quality across the product. Below are the four workstreams I initiated based on gaps I identified, not work handed down to me.

Design Language System

No shared component library existed. Each module had been built independently, compounding inconsistency platform-wide. I proposed, scoped, and built the Calcite DLS from scratch — 90+ components, with documentation that now serves as the blueprint for the planned Storybook transition.

RBAC Access Management Dashboard

Early interviews revealed analysts had no visibility into their own permission boundaries, generating repeated PM escalations with no tool to resolve them. I made the case to reprioritise, then designed the dashboard end to end. Now used by 1,000+ government users.

UX Research Practice

Testing was ad-hoc and findings weren't reaching the product team in an actionable form. I introduced two frameworks and ran 30+ interviews across 7 features. GLS research with 6 stakeholders directly caused a roadmap reprioritisation: one feature fast-tracked, another deferred.

Design files were unnavigable outside of my immediate context. I restructured the Figma workspace so the URA team and engineering teams could reference design decisions independently, without routing through me.


Beyond these workstreams, I also collaborated with engineers through grooming and QA cycles to ensure design intent was faithfully implemented, and delivered UX flows across 10+ features including Site Simulation, Local Traffic Simulation, and Government Land Sales.

Design system governance

ePlanner's team had been building on Calcite, Esri's open-source design system. The problem was that Calcite was designed for general GIS tooling, not for ePlanner's specialised custom interfaces. With no shared process for handling gaps, each team solved the same problems independently, resulting in inconsistent components scattered across modules. Thus I came up with a plan to consolidate ePlanner's design system, to ensure it's scalability and consistency across all the tools.

Rather than patch inconsistencies one at a time, I proposed a structured DLS overhaul to management and got alignment before starting. The goal was to make the library a true single source of truth: something the team could hand to an engineer and trust they'd build the right thing, with or without a designer in the room.

DLS enhancement plan


The work happened in three phases: audit and clean up unused Calcite components; build ePlanner-specific components that Calcite never covered; then document everything with usage guidelines, anatomy breakdowns, and interaction rules. The mid-project reality check was that the DLS I'd initially organised was already becoming unwieldy as the team grew — I ran a second pass to restructure it around Atomic Design principles before it became unmaintainable.

01. Restructured the Figma library

Reorganised the component library to follow Atomic Design principles, replacing a flat, hard-to-navigate structure with intuitive categories including Templates, Navigation, Cards, and Patterns.

02. Tidied existing Calcite components

Audited and cleaned Calcite components to better fit ePlanner's use cases. Removed unused variants, simplified prop sets, and brought component structures in line with how the platform actually uses them.

03. Built new custom components

Designed ePlanner-specific components that fall outside Calcite's scope entirely, including Notice Banners, Button Group (Round), ePlanner Navigation, Workspace Card, and Content Display. Each follows consistent patterns for use across all tools.

04. Adding documentation

Added usage guidelines, anatomy breakdowns, spacing rules, and contextual examples for all updated and new components. These docs serve as the blueprint for the eventual Storybook transition.

05. Established shared design patterns

Beyond individual components, I reviewed tools across ePlanner and defined fixed interaction patterns to ensure consistent behaviour across all modules — covering panel layouts, left panel interactions, tool-switching flows, and AI-assisted features like Image Generation.

06. Documenting inconsistencies to be fixed

I also noted down any inconsistencies in existing interactions, and ensured alignment with both the design and development teams on the new consistent interaction to be used moving forward.


With the DLS in place, engineering teams across modules could build against a shared reference for the first time. New feature builds no longer required my direct involvement on every component decision — teams could work independently while staying visually and behaviourally consistent. The documentation I wrote also serves as the blueprint for the planned Storybook transition, which will move the source of truth from Figma into a developer-owned environment the team controls entirely.

RBAC: Access management for ePlanner

ePlanner's datasets carry varying levels of sensitivity. Different government agencies and user groups need access to different datasets, and that access has to be managed, audited, and kept current. Before RBAC, this was handled manually and inconsistently — admins had no structured way to assign access at scale, users had no visibility into what they could access or why, and group owners were fielding ad hoc requests with no centralised tool to manage them.

RBAC is a standalone access management system built alongside ePlanner to solve this. It serves four distinct user roles, each with meaningfully different needs, and handles two core flows: automated access assignment through Standing Orders, and manual access through user-initiated requests.

Admin

Creates and manages groups, submits Standing Order requests for automated agency-based assignment, monitors onboarding metrics, and handles new agency domains flagged by the system.

Manager

Reviews and approves or rejects Admin's Standing Order requests, with access to supporting evidence and the ability to request modifications before making a final decision.

Group Owner

Reviews individual access requests for groups they own, approves or denies with comments, can bulk-action multiple requests, set approval delegates, and view request history.

User

Requests access to groups from within ePlanner or directly via RBAC, tracks request status, views current memberships, and receives notifications on approval outcomes.

Standing Orders: automated assignment

The core challenge for admins was scaling access management across agencies. Manually adding individual users was unsustainable as ePlanner grew. Standing Orders solve this by letting admins define rules. For example, any user whose agency matches "URA" is automatically assigned to the URA group on first login. The flow required careful design to handle edge cases: what happens when an agency is new and has no existing Standing Order, when a Manager rejects an Admin's request, or when a user logs in for the first time from an unknown domain.

Group access management

User Requests: self-serve access

Users can also discover datasets they need access to, either from within ePlanner directly or by browsing RBAC's group directory, and submit a request. The design challenge here was the form complexity. A single user request for multiple groups generates separate requests to each group owner, each of whom acts independently. Users needed clear visibility into the status of each request without the interface becoming overwhelming. Group owners needed to be able to act quickly across multiple pending requests without losing context on who was requesting what and why.

Establishing a research practice

URA had run user sessions before, but without structure. Findings varied in format, weren't traceable to specific design decisions, and rarely reached the product team in a form they could act on. Before running any research, I created and presented a moderated UT protocol and synthesis template to management, making the case for why systematic testing was worth the cost. Management's buy-in came from that presentation, not from asking for permission.

Introducing a new process


The first formal study — Workspace and Site Simulation UT with 9 users — validated that the new Workspace landing page improved usability, and that the redesigned Site Simulation flow was more intuitive than what it replaced. Specific findings fed directly into the next sprint priorities. The synthesis format I used became the template for every subsequent study.

The most consequential research was the Government Land Sales study. GLS is a high-stakes process — before a land parcel goes to public tender, URA teams run detailed simulations to assess viability. I interviewed 6 senior planners to map exactly how they used ePlanner in this workflow, ran concept tests on existing tools, and surfaced a significant gap: a core GLS preparation step had no proper ePlanner support, forcing planners to work around the tool instead of with it. Two features that had been on the roadmap were deprioritised. GLS support was fast-tracked. That shift came from the research, not from internal assumptions.
